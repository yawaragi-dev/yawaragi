# Yawaragi Feature Design Research: Journal, Ratings, Restaurant/Shop Choosing, Grounded Insights, and Roadmap

## TL;DR
- **Build a fast quick-log journal + Bayesian-shrunk ratings first, then a curated-corpus insight engine — in that order.** The highest-leverage, lowest-risk features slot cleanly into your existing infrastructure: the journal feeds your EMA taste vector new signals (temperature, price, season), ratings need IMDb-style Bayesian shrinkage to be honest at cold start, and "genuinely interesting insights" should be a **hybrid** of zero-hallucination computed facts from the Sakenowa dataset plus a small RAG-over-curated-corpus with mandatory quote-level citations.
- **The restaurant menu-scanner and shop shelf-scanner are your portfolio-differentiating showpieces but also your biggest scope traps.** Vivino proved menu OCR is useful even when imperfect ("gravy," in one reviewer's words); the honest, provenance-tracked graceful-degradation UX ("not in our database" + a guided sommelier-question fallback) is what distinguishes you from the "SAKE AI" free-text competitor. Multi-bottle shelf recognition is genuinely hard — ship single-label scan first.
- **Explicitly do NOT build social features, a buy-now marketplace, or a real-time brewery-availability database.** They carry GDPR/moderation burden, contradict your non-promotional stance, and have no reliable data source. Sake-specific wins no competitor does well — a **temperature-experiment tracker** and **opened-bottle freshness timer** — are cheap, native, and uniquely yours.

## Key Findings

### 1. Drink-journaling UX conventions are well-established; sake needs extra fields
The dominant pattern across Untappd, Vivino, and the Japanese sake apps is a **two-tier log**: a friction-free "quick check-in" (identify + optional star) and an expandable "deep log" (notes, serving style, location, purchase location, photo, flavor tags, friends). Untappd's own help docs confirm a check-in can be as minimal as identifying the beer — "You do not need to leave a rating! It's perfectly fine to take a qualitative approach" — with optional photo, tasting notes, rating, serving style, location, purchased location, and flavor profiles. Untappd's data export has 32 fields per check-in; it separates **location** (where consumed) from **purchased location** (store).

The Japanese sake apps validate the sake-specific fields: **Sakenote** ("Rate drinks on a scale of 5 — Store pictures of labels or side dishes together — Location can be automatically recorded," [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/sakenote-japanese-sake-tasting-note/id556305580) and it works offline); **Sakenomy** ("make a note of your tasting comments, **the temperature at which you drank it**, and upload a photo of the bottle. Recording the restaurant and date"). [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=jp.co.mti.sakenomy&hl=en_US) The recurring community insight (via The Bridge's write-up of Sakenote) is to photograph the *tsumami* (snack/food) alongside the label because it "can help to trigger your memory later." [BRIDGE](https://thebridge.jp/en/2013/06/sakenote)

**Sake-specific journal fields the research supports capturing:** serving temperature (the named 10-tier ladder — yuki-hie 5°C, hana-bie 10°C, suzu-hie 15°C, [SAKETIMES](https://en.sake-times.com/learn/sake-101-serving-temperatures) hiya/jō-on ~20°C, hinata-kan 30°C, hitohada-kan 35°C, nuru-kan 40°C, jō-kan 45°C, atsukan 50°C, [MADE in JAPAN.JP](https://made-in-japan.jp/en/guide/sake-temperature/) tobikiri-kan 55°C); vessel (ochoko / wine glass / masu / tokkuri); food pairing; occasion; location; purchase location; price paid; glass-vs-bottle; brewery year (BY); seasonal designation (shiboritate, hiyaoroshi, nama); and home-aging notes. The temperature ladder is a genuine sake-native differentiator — the British Sake Association and SAKETIMES both document it, and NRIB guidance ties temperature to style (fragrant ginjo/nama chilled ~10°C; mild sake 35–40°C; aged sake 42–45°C).

**Retention mechanics:** Duolingo's streak playbook is the reference. The mechanism is loss aversion; the humane escape valve (streak freeze) matters more than the streak itself. Trophy's Duolingo gamification case study reports that "among daily streak users who have passed seven days, those on apps with streak freeze functionality average 17.19 days on streak, compared to 11.62 days for those without — a 48% diff[erence]." But a caution: a well-documented failure mode is hollow engagement, where "people weren't logging in to learn anymore. They were logging in so they didn't lose." [Justanotherpm](https://www.justanotherpm.com/blog/the-psychology-behind-duolingos-streak-feature) For a reflective hobby like sake, favour gentle stats and a yearly recap over aggressive daily streaks.

### 2. Rating scale: keep 5-star for input, but fix aggregation with Bayesian shrinkage
Vivino (5-star), Untappd (0.25 increments, or 0.10 for "Insiders"), and CellarTracker (100-point) span the spectrum. The Japanese sake apps (Sakenote, Sakenomy, Sakenowa) all use 5-star. Vivino publicly claims its 5-point scale "strongly correlates with the 100-point system used by most critics" [Vivino](https://www.vivino.com/en/app) and that a 4.0 Vivino rating correlates with a 90-point expert rating. [WineFamily](https://wine-family.com/pages/the-vivino-5-star-rating-system-what-you-need-to-know) The 100-point scale, while beloved by critics, compresses almost everything into 85–100 (a bell curve around 87–89), which is poor for novices. **Recommendation: 5-star with half-star increments for input** — familiar, low-cognitive-load, and it maps cleanly onto your existing EMA weight formula.

The critical fix is **display aggregation at cold start**. With 1–3 ratings per sake, a raw average is misleading. The IMDb weighted-rating (true Bayesian estimate) formula is the standard remedy: **WR = (v÷(v+m))·R + (m÷(v+m))·C**, [IMDb](https://help.imdb.com/article/imdb/track-movies-tv/ratings-faq/G67Y87TFYYP6TWAV) where R = the sake's mean, v = its number of ratings, m = a minimum-ratings prior, and C = the global mean across all sake. Items with few ratings are pulled toward the global mean. For your tiny user base, set C to your platform-wide mean and m to a small constant (e.g., 3–5); show the shrunk value and always display the rating count (and ideally a confidence indicator) so a single 5-star doesn't read as "the best sake in the database."

**Rating→taste-vector interaction.** Your current design (weight = (rating−3)/5) is reasonable but improvable with two research-backed adjustments: (a) **recency/context weighting** — a rating logged "at a party" (a context field on the journal entry) should update the taste vector with lower weight than a "focused tasting," because context demonstrably distorts perception (the wine literature shows price knowledge alone raises scores; Vivino reviewers note palate drift over time skews old ratings). (b) Keep EMA for the vector but consider a **Dirichlet/Bayesian prior** if you ever surface per-user "predicted rating." Multi-dimensional rating (rating each of the 6 axes) is tempting but the research favours **overall star + optional flavor tags** (Untappd's model) for novices — per-axis sliders are expert-grade and increase logging friction. You already have the 6-axis vectors from Sakenowa; let users *confirm/adjust* the axis rather than rate from scratch.

### 3. Choosing sake in a restaurant: menu-scan + honest fallback + deterministic pairing
Vivino's wine-list scanner (launched November 2014) is the proven template. Per diginomica's technical write-up, it "relies on optical character recognition (OCR) technology, ABBYY... the image is compressed and sent from their phone or tablet to the ABBYY FineReader Engine system installed on Vivino's back-end server," where text is extracted, keywords matched against the database, and results superimposed on the original menu image. Its stated purpose is squarely your use case — VP Torben Mottes said the target is "the average wine consumer... in restaurants where there might not be a sommelier," and users "feel intimidated talking to the sommelier" and lack "the right words to say what I like." Its real-world reliability is partial and users know it: one reviewer's verdict — "This works...sometimes. It will really depend on how the wine list is structured... Sometimes the optical character recognition will only pick up part of the wine's identification and return a wine that is completely off... If it works at all, it is better than having absolutely no information."

**Realistic match rate for sake in Germany/EU:** The frozen (Feb 2024) Sakenowa mirror will cover major exported brands (Dassai, Dewazakura, Kubota, etc.) but the community complaints show coverage is the #1 pain point — a Sakenomy reviewer: "the bottles around my house aren't yet in the existing database"; [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/sakenomy/id917958935) a Sakenowa reviewer running a sushi restaurant: "the app doesn't recognize any sake on my menu... none of anything I have or know of is in this app." Your `brand_overrides` manual-curation layer is exactly the right architecture. **The graceful-degradation UX is the differentiator:** for each menu line, return one of three provenance-tagged states — (1) matched to Sakenowa/override with taste-vector rank, (2) partially matched (brand known, specific expression unknown), (3) honestly "not in our database." This honesty is your anti-hallucination moat versus SAKE AI's free-text Gemini output.

**Sommelier-question fallback.** When match fails, offer a guided "ask the staff these 3 questions" flow — e.g., "Is it more fruity/floral (ginjo-type) or rich/rice-forward (junmai/kimoto)?", "Dry or with some sweetness?", "Do you recommend it chilled or warm?" This maps their answers onto your 6 axes and ranks accordingly. No surveyed app does guided-question fallbacks well; this is a genuine gap.

**Food pairing as a deterministic table (not LLM-generated).** The pairing literature is remarkably consistent and encodable, mirroring your existing `mapCrossBeverage` table. Core principles from WSET (Natsuki Kikuya), Decanter, and Wine Enthusiast: sake is low-acid, tannin-free, and umami-rich. The Japan Sake and Shochu Makers Association (JSS) states the magnitudes directly: "The acidity of sake is about 1/5th of wine... Sake is rich in amino acids, containing over five times more than wine and beer. Amino acids and peptides produce the umami flavor found in sake." Sake pairs by **resonance** (like-with-like) rather than contrast — Akashi-Tai's educator: "Finding a similar profile will make tastes double. It is not one plus one equals two with sake... It's one plus one equals ten." Encodable rules: junmai/honjozo = versatile all-rounders; ginjo/daiginjo (high f1) = delicate, chilled, with seafood/scallops/caviar; kimoto/yamahai (high umami) = cheese, mushrooms, cured meats, hot pot, and respond well to warming; the lactic-acid affinity means sake pairs with dairy/fermented foods. Temperature is a pairing lever unique to sake — warming raises perceived sweetness and umami while softening acidity. A deterministic food→style→axis table gives zero-hallucination, explainable pairing.

**Decision speed under social pressure.** Vivino's product framing stresses time-to-answer ("all of this loads in the time it takes to pick up the bottle"). For party ordering, surface a single top pick + one alternative + a one-line rationale, not a ranked list of ten.

### 4. Choosing sake in a shop: single-label scan first, barcode as a reliability upgrade, shelf-scan later
Vivino's shelf/"quick compare" scan (Premium) lets users scan several bottles in succession and compare side by side; the full multi-bottle-in-one-image detection is technically demanding. The research shows multi-object retail-shelf recognition is a specialized CV problem (YOLO-family detectors, similarity thresholds, multi-angle capture), and general multimodal LLMs are imperfect at fine subtype identification. The peer-reviewed study *Use of Multimodal Artificial Intelligence in Surgical Instrument Recognition* (Bioengineering 2025, 12(1):72) found ChatGPT-4o scored 89.1% on category-level identification but only 33.69% on precise subtype naming (a specialized commercial app, SID 2.0, reached 39.1% on subtype): "For precise subtype identification of instrument names (like 'Mayo scissors' or 'Kelly forceps'), all models had low accuracy." The category-vs-subtype gap is the relevant analogy: identifying "a bottle of sake" is easy; identifying *which specific expression* from a shelf photo is hard. **Implication: single-label scan (your Phase 3) will be far more reliable than shelf scan.** Ship single-label first; treat shelf-scan as a post-launch "scan each bottle quickly" carousel rather than true simultaneous detection.

**German/EU shop context:** The realistic retail universe is specialist importers and shops — UENO GOURMET (Germany/Austria/Switzerland's leading importer since 2005, [UENO GOURMET](https://de.japan-gourmet.com/en/shop/sake/) 30+ breweries, brands incl. Dassai, Dewazakura, Amabuki, Masuizumi, Yuki no Bosha), [Sake Guide](https://sakeguide.net/articles/2019/8/30/where-to-buy-sake) Sake Kontor Berlin (since 2004/ [Cee Cee](https://ceecee.cc/en/sake-kontor-berlins-erster-sake-shop/) 2010, classic brands Masumi, Kenbishi), [Sake Guide](https://sakeguide.net/articles/2019/8/30/where-to-buy-sake) Sake36 Berlin (modern styles Aramasa, Kaze no Mori, Senkin), [Sake Guide](https://sakeguide.net/articles/2019/8/30/where-to-buy-sake) plus Japanese groceries and a thin supermarket presence. Much of this is a curated import SKU set — meaning your override layer must cover the specific expressions these importers carry, which is a tractable manual-curation target (dozens, not thousands, of high-frequency SKUs).

**Barcode scanning is a more reliable ID than OCR where data exists.** Open Food Facts is free, no API key, ODbL-licensed, with "excellent coverage of European EAN codes" [GitHub](https://github.com/sysadminsmedia/homebox/discussions/1405) and an alcohol field on wines/beers — but sake coverage is crowdsourced and likely sparse; treat a barcode hit as a high-confidence bonus path (EAN → brand match) and fall back to label OCR when absent. Budget/value filtering ("best value under €X") and a **wishlist** (save bottles to find later) are low-effort, high-value; Vivino's wishlist is one of its stickiest features.

### 5. Grounded insights: a HYBRID of computed data-facts + citation-locked RAG is the right architecture
This is the hardest and most important section, and the answer is architectural, not editorial.

**Layer A — Zero-hallucination computed insights from the Sakenowa dataset.** The official Sakenowa Data API (muro.sakenowa.com/sakenowa-data/, [sakenowa](https://muro.sakenowa.com/sakenowa-data/) Aiiro Systems Inc., free, JSON/UTF-8, no key required) has these endpoints and fields: `/areas` (id, name — the 47 prefectures); `/breweries` (id, name, areaId); `/brands` (id, name, breweryId — nullable); [sakenowa](https://muro.sakenowa.com/sakenowa-data/) `/flavor-charts` → key `flavorCharts` (brandId, f1–f6 as 0–1 floats); `/flavor-tags` (id, name); `/brand-flavor-tags` → key `flavorTags` (brandId, tagIds[]); `/rankings` (yearMonth, overall[50], areas[]). The join chain **Brand.breweryId → Brewery.areaId → Area** lets you compute, with **zero hallucination risk**: breweries/brands per prefecture; the driest brand/region (max/avg f5); most fruity/heavy/light region (f1/f3/f6 aggregates); flavor-tag frequency by region. These are "data storytelling" auto-insights — each is a computation over canonical data, so provenance = `sakenowa` with a computed flag.

**Critical constraints verified in the API docs:** there is **no founding-year/date field** anywhere in the API (Brewery = id/name/areaId only). So the tempting insight "this is one of the oldest breweries, founded in X" is **NOT** zero-hallucination computable and must not be generated — it would require an external verified dataset. Likewise, `/rankings` scores use an undisclosed algorithm; display "most popular per Sakenowa" but derive no quantitative claims from `score`. Note also that not every brand has a flavor chart (many are missing), so aggregates must handle absence, and the license requires **attribution with a link to sakenowa.com** — the official example string is `<a href="https://sakenowa.com">さけのわデータ</a>を利用しています` — which your existing `<SakenowaAttribution />` component already handles.

**Layer B — Curated-corpus RAG with mandatory quote-level citations, for editorial "did-you-know."** For richer insights (history, brewing science, seasonality), build a small provenance-tracked knowledge base where every insight links to its source span. The research strongly supports **extractive grounding over paraphrase**: the FRONT framework (arXiv 2408.04568) shows LLMs should "extract supporting quotes from the source documents, each associated with its document identifier," [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.04568) which "significantly reduces the impact of... irrelevant information and the risk of hallucinations." The danger to design against is "citation-shaped hallucinations" — "answers that appear grounded because they include quotes, links... while the actual claim is unsupported." Mitigation: retrieve → extract verbatim quote + source ID → validate the quote actually exists in the source (string match) → only then render, with the quote and a link shown to the user. This is a natural fit for your provenance model (`source` field + confidence + UI badge) and your Vercel AI SDK `generateObject`+Zod stack.

**Legally usable sources (EU/German law).** German quotation right §51 UrhG (Zitatrecht) permits reproducing published-work excerpts *for the purpose of quotation*, but with strict conditions verified in the research: the work must be already published; [Orca](https://www.orca.nrw/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/RiDHnrw_26-11-20_Das-Zitatrecht-nach-P-51-UrhG_cc.pdf) there must be a **Zitatzweck** — an intrinsic content connection where the quote supports/illustrates your own independent commentary [University of Göttingen](https://www.uni-goettingen.de/en/right+of+quotation,+section+51+urhg/126523.html) (a "simple copy" to "accumulate content" is NOT covered even with attribution); [Rechtsanwalt Ferner](https://www.ferner-alsdorf.de/urheberrecht-das-zitatrecht-nach-%C2%A751-urhg-zitierfreiheit-zitatzweck-rechtsanwalt/) the quote must be no longer than the purpose justifies (Kleinzitat = small excerpts); the source and author **must** be named (§63 UrhG); [Rechtsanwalt Ferner](https://www.ferner-alsdorf.de/urheberrecht-das-zitatrecht-nach-%C2%A751-urhg-zitierfreiheit-zitatzweck-rechtsanwalt/) and the work may not be altered (§62). [Orca](https://www.orca.nrw/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/RiDHnrw_26-11-20_Das-Zitatrecht-nach-P-51-UrhG_cc.pdf) **Practical implication:** you may quote short, attributed excerpts from SAKETIMES, Japan Times, Nippon.com, WSET blogs, etc. *only* when wrapped in your own explanatory text — not as standalone reproduced content. Safer, cleaner sources: **UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage listing** (traditional knowledge and skills of sake-making with kōji mold, inscribed 4 December 2024 at the 19th session/19.COM of the Intergovernmental Committee in Asunción, Paraguay; official RL entry 01977) has quotable official descriptions ("Originally, sake was made only by women. As demand increased, men became involved in the process. Today, people of all genders can master the knowledge and skills"); NRIB and JSS educational materials; brewery websites' own kura stories (with permission/attribution); and Open Food Facts (ODbL). John Gauntner's Sake World is authoritative [Sakeeducationcouncil](https://sakeeducationcouncil.net/personnel/john-gauntner/) but copyrighted — link and short-quote only, never wholesale ingest. Prefer linking + short quotation + your own synthesis over reproduction.

**Micro-content design.** The "daily insight / did-you-know" pattern works when it feels valuable, not spammy — Duolingo-style micro-content succeeds via personalization and progress feeling. Tie each daily card to the user's own journal (e.g., "You've logged 4 junmai but no kimoto — here's why kimoto tastes different") or to seasonality. **Seasonality is your best evergreen structured-content framework**: the sake calendar (shiboritate/winter new-pressed, hanami sake/spring, hiyaoroshi/autumn) is factual, recurring, and computable against the current date with zero hallucination.

### 6. Other high-value features the research surfaced
- **Opened-bottle freshness tracker (uniquely sake-native, trivially cheap).** The literature converges: opened sake is best within ~3 days, [Myjapaneseworld](https://myjapaneseworld.com/blogs/japanese-craftsman-journal/does-sake-go-bad-1) good ~1 week refrigerated, up to ~2 weeks with a vacuum stopper (per Foodie: "if the bottle is capped and refrigerated, sake can be drunk up to two weeks after opening. Still, it's best to consume sake within a week (or even a few days)"); nama (unpasteurized), ginjo/daiginjo need refrigeration even unopened; [The Takeout](https://www.thetakeout.com/1645986/how-long-store-open-sake/) the label date is bottling, not expiry. [Tippsy Sake](https://www.tippsysake.com/blogs/tippsy-sake-blog/does-sake-go-bad) A "bottle opened on X" timer with a style-aware freshness estimate is a delightful, data-grounded feature no major app does well.
- **Temperature-experiment tracker (the standout sake-native idea).** Community sources explicitly frame temperature exploration as the core ritual — UrbanSake: "the name of the game is to experiment with temperature and find what you like." [UrbanSake](https://www.urbansake.com/sake-101/sake-temperature/) Let a user log the *same* sake at multiple temperatures with a note each, then visualize how their preference shifts. This also feeds a **temperature-preference signal** into the taste profile — a signal no competitor captures.
- **Cellar/inventory management** (bottles owned, with the freshness timer above) — a natural pairing with the wishlist.
- **Tasting-flight / blind-tasting mode** (compare 2–4 sakes side by side; hide names for blind mode) — leverages your flavor-radar component directly.
- **Vocabulary-learning mode** — turn your existing glossary into spaced-repetition flashcards (the temperature ladder, label terms like polishing ratio/seimaibuai, kimoto/yamahai). Low effort, high learn-value, and reinforces retention without aggressive streaks.
- **Brewery-visit travel mode for Japan trips** — appealing for your traveler audience, but flag: there is **no single reliable structured dataset** of visitable breweries with tasting rooms; this would be heavy manual curation. Scope as a lightweight "breweries by prefecture" browser (computable from Sakenowa `/breweries`+`/areas`) rather than a live tourism directory.
- **GDPR data export (CSV/JSON of journal)** — a portability requirement anyway, and cheap; Untappd's 32-field export is the model.
- **"My top sakes" shareable card** — an image card of a user's top-rated sake, exportable/shareable, mirroring the streak-share acquisition loop, but without building social infrastructure.
- **Corrections/community loop → journal.** Your existing `corrections` table ("was this scan correct?") can quietly improve match rates over time; keep it as structured correction, not free-form social.

### Features to explicitly NOT build (scope traps for a solo dev at ~10 hrs/week)
- **Social network / feed / follows / comments.** GDPR + moderation + cold-start-community death spiral. The Japanese apps' "Live/kanpai" social features are largely unused outside Japan (Taste Translation observed the Sakenote live feature "seems to be only used in Japanese"). [Taste-translation](https://taste-translation.com/japanese-sake-apps/) Skip entirely.
- **Buy-now marketplace / e-commerce.** Contradicts your non-promotional constraint (discover/learn/explore, not buy-now) and adds payment/legal complexity.
- **Real-time German retailer availability/price data.** No structured source exists; it would be perpetual manual curation that goes stale. A static wishlist + "which importers carry this brand" (manually curated, low-frequency) is the sustainable version.
- **True simultaneous multi-bottle shelf detection.** Technically hard, low reliability; the single-scan carousel achieves 90% of the value.
- **Founding-year / brewery-age insights** from Sakenowa alone (no such field — hallucination risk).
- **Per-axis mandatory rating on every log** — friction that will kill logging completion.

## Recommendations (staged roadmap slotting into existing phases)

**Phase 5.5 (immediately after taste profile — ship the core loop):**
1. **Journal (quick-log + deep-log)** with the sake-specific fields and temperature ladder. This is the backbone; everything feeds it. *Benchmark to proceed:* quick-log completion in under ~15 seconds.
2. **Bayesian rating display (IMDb WR)** with visible rating counts. Ship *before* any public launch — misleading averages at cold start are a trust and (arguably) consumer-fairness problem.
3. **Opened-bottle freshness timer** and **temperature-experiment tracker** — cheap, sake-native, delightful, and both feed new taste-profile signals.
4. **Context-weighted EMA** (party vs. focused tasting) — a small change with real accuracy payoff.

**Phase 6.5 (after evals/Langfuse infra exists — the showpieces):**
5. **Restaurant menu scanner** with 3-state provenance results + sommelier-question fallback. This is the recruiter-visible engineering centerpiece; the eval harness from Phase 6 (golden sets, LLM-as-judge) directly de-risks it. *Benchmark:* on a golden set of ~30 real German-Japanese-restaurant menu photos, measure match rate; if under ~50%, lead with the guided-question fallback rather than the scanner.
6. **Deterministic food-pairing table** — pairs with the menu scanner and is fully explainable.
7. **Layer A computed insights** + **seasonality cards** — zero-hallucination, immediately shippable once the Sakenowa mirror exists.

**Post-launch (only if usage justifies):**
8. **Curated-corpus RAG (Layer B)** with quote-validation — the most engineering-intensive; do it only once you have the eval infra and a small, legally-vetted corpus. Start with ~50 hand-picked, license-cleared source snippets, not a web crawl.
9. **Shop barcode + wishlist**, **vocab flashcards**, **GDPR export**, **shareable card**, **flight mode**, **prefecture brewery browser**.

**Portfolio-differentiating (recruiter-visible engineering) vs. pure user-value:**
- *Portfolio-differentiating:* menu scanner (multimodal + structured extraction + graceful degradation), hybrid insight architecture (computed + citation-validated RAG), Bayesian aggregation, provenance/eval discipline. These demonstrate senior judgment — especially the anti-hallucination guarantees.
- *Pure user-value (still worth it, low effort):* freshness timer, temperature tracker, wishlist, flashcards, seasonality cards, export.

**Anti-hallucination architecture — explicit recommendation:** Adopt the **hybrid**. Default every insight to **Layer A (computed from canonical Sakenowa data)** whenever the claim is a count, aggregate, or axis comparison — these carry zero hallucination risk and need only a "computed from Sakenowa" badge. Use **Layer B (RAG)** only for genuinely editorial content, and enforce **extractive grounding with runtime quote-validation**: the model must return a verbatim span + source ID, your code must string-match that span against the stored source before rendering, and the UI must show the quote and link. Never allow a Layer-B insight to render without a validated citation. ## Caveats

Treat "match rate" numbers as things you must measure on your own golden set.
