# Cross-Beverage Map: Research

> **Purpose.** This document feeds the hand-curated `CrossBeverageMap` table that bridges Western beverage descriptors to positions on Sakenowa's 6-axis `FlavorProfile`. It is the source material for project issue **#140** and is the input the maintainers will use when populating `src/lib/ai/tools/map-cross-beverage.ts` (the local AI SDK tool referenced in [ADR-0003 amendment 2026-06-05](../adr/0003-mcp-server-extractability.md) and [ADR-0014](../adr/0014-manual-curation-and-source-strategy.md)).
>
> **Provenance contract.** Every row produced by this table is emitted with `source: "cross_beverage_map"` per [ADR-0005](../adr/0005-source-provenance.md). In the UI, results render with **both** `<ProvenanceBadge />` and `<HeuristicDisclaimer />`. The disclaimer ("Cross-beverage mappings are approximations. Western descriptors like 'smoky' or 'tannic' have no direct sake equivalent.") is non-negotiable.
>
> **Posture.** This is heuristic, not science. Sake's flavor axes are sake-specific; Western descriptors do not translate cleanly. The reasoning column states the *shared sensory dimension* on which the mapping rests, and where the analogy breaks down. Where no published source supports a row, the reasoning column says so explicitly.

## Methodology

### Sources consulted

The mapping draws on five tiers of source material, weighted in the order they appear in [CLAUDE.md](../../CLAUDE.md)'s "Source(s)" guidance:

1. **John Gauntner's writing** at [sake-world.com](https://sake-world.com), including the *Sake Flavor Profiles* primer and the public *Sake World* glossary. Gauntner remains the most-cited Western voice on sake style language.
2. **Sake Times** ([en.sake-times.com](https://en.sake-times.com)) — Japan-based English-language publication. Notably its "Classic Wine Pairings Meet Sake" series, which gives explicit sake-as-wine-substitute mappings with brand names.
3. **Sake Street** ([sakestreet.com](https://sakestreet.com)) — particularly its bodaimoto / mizumoto coverage, which is essential for the natural-wine analogues.
4. **The Whisky Advocate's** ["The Whisky Lover's Guide to Sake"](https://whiskyadvocate.com/the-whisky-lovers-guide-to-sake) — the single most useful source for the whisky table. It supplies five direct style-to-style pairings with brand and reasoning.
5. **Sakenowa's own published documentation** at [muro.sakenowa.com/posts/flavor-chart](https://muro.sakenowa.com/posts/flavor-chart/) and the [Sakenowa Data API guide](https://muro.sakenowa.com/sakenowa-data). These are the ground truth for the numeric axes.
6. **Trade press and sommelier sites** — *Whisky Advocate*, *VinePair*, *Wine Enthusiast*, *Decanter*, *Time Out*, *Wine & Spirit Education Trust* (WSET), *Skurnik*, *The Koji Club*, *Brewsake*, *Sake Sommelier Association* materials, and *True Sake's* curation copy. Treated as Tier-3 supporting evidence.
7. **Sake brewery and retailer brand pages** — used only to anchor flavor descriptions for individual sake brands (Dassai, Born, Kikuhime, Tengumai, Daishichi, Niida Honke, Senkin, Tedorigawa, Hakkaisan, Kubota, Suigei, etc.), not for cross-domain claims.

Books we leaned on conceptually (cited without page numbers — confirmation of style, not extraction of numeric values):

- John Gauntner, *Sake Confidential* (Stone Bridge, 2014)
- John Gauntner, *The Sake Handbook* (Tuttle, 2002)
- Beau Timken, *Sake: A Modern Guide* (Chronicle, 2007)
- Anthony Rose, *Sake and the Wines of Japan* (Infinite Ideas / Classic Wine Library, 2018)

### Methodology in three steps

1. **Anchor the Western beverage's sensory profile.** Use widely-cited descriptors from trade press (sweetness, body, aromatic intensity, oxidation level, residual sugar, peat/smoke, phenolic character) — not flavor wheels invented for this doc.
2. **Translate dimension-by-dimension into the Sakenowa axes.** Each Western descriptor is examined against each of the six axes, asking only "does this dimension transfer, and if so, with what loss?" — never "would the user like both".
3. **Pick the closest sake style + a representative brand.** Sake style first (Junmai / Ginjo / Daiginjo / Kimoto / Yamahai / Koshu / Nigori / Taruzake / Sparkling), then the brand within that style with the most stable published profile.

### Source-quality discipline

- Wherever a published source directly supports the mapping, the **Source(s)** column cites it.
- Wherever the mapping is a derivation from established sake-style attributes plus the Sakenowa axis definitions, the cell reads `[derived from Sakenowa axis defs + style notes]`.
- Wherever no source is available and the mapping is heuristic-only, the cell reads `[no published source — heuristic from Sakenowa-axis definitions only]`. These rows are honest about being editorial calls.

## How the 6 axes were calibrated

The Sakenowa axes are derived from Japanese-language tasting language and from user-submitted comments on the Sakenowa platform. Each axis is `[0, 1]`. Per [Sakenowa's documentation](https://muro.sakenowa.com/posts/flavor-chart/), "強さは銘柄ごとに調整していますので他の銘柄と比較することはできません" — the strength values are normalised per brand, so absolute cross-brand comparison is imperfect. For the purposes of this map, we treat each axis as a relative position on a 0..1 scale where `0.00` means "absent / not characteristic" and `1.00` means "definitional / extreme exemplar".

Axis-by-axis calibration anchors:

- **f1 hanayaka (華やか, fragrant / floral).** Driven by aromatic esters (banana, melon, pear, green apple) produced by low-temperature ginjo fermentation with Kyokai #1801 / #9 yeasts. A typical Junmai Daiginjo sits 0.70–0.95. Junmai or Honjozo without ginjo aromatics sits 0.10–0.30. Kimoto and Yamahai usually 0.05–0.25 (their aromatic signature is earthy, not floral). When mapping a wine, "perfumed Riesling" → high f1; "burgundian terroir Pinot" → low f1.
- **f2 hojun (芳醇, mellow / rich).** Umami + aroma depth. Driven by amino acid content. Junmai > Honjozo > Ginjo > Daiginjo on f2 typically. Kimoto and Yamahai are the f2 champions (often 0.75–0.95). Koshu is also high (0.70–0.90). When mapping a wine, "oaked Chardonnay leesy" → moderately high f2; "lean Muscadet" → low f2.
- **f3 juko (重厚, heavy / full-bodied).** Weight + amino acid concentration; not tannin. Genshu (undiluted), Kimoto, Yamahai, and Junmai often sit 0.60–0.90 on f3. Daiginjo and Honjozo are typically 0.10–0.30. When mapping "heavy Cabernet Sauvignon" → high f3, but the *reason* is body, not tannin (there is no tannin axis in sake).
- **f4 odayaka (穏やか, mild / calm).** Restrained aroma, not "neutral". A Hakkaisan-style Niigata tanrei karakuchi sake sits 0.55–0.80 on f4. Aromatic Daiginjo sits 0.10–0.30 (the opposite). When mapping "minerally Muscadet" or "Lowland Scotch" → moderate-high f4.
- **f5 dry (ドライ, dry).** Closest 1:1 to Western descriptors; correlates with SMV (sake meter value). Karakuchi sake sits 0.70–0.95. Sweet kijoshu or sweet nigori sits 0.05–0.25. When mapping "bone-dry Chablis" or "Brut Nature Champagne" → high f5; "Sauternes" or "PX sherry" → low f5.
- **f6 keikai (軽快, light / crisp).** Refreshing finish, low residual. Ginjo and Daiginjo at the dry end are high f6 (0.65–0.90). Junmai, Kimoto, Yamahai, and Koshu are low f6 (0.05–0.25). When mapping "Pilsner" or "Sauvignon Blanc" → high f6; "Imperial Stout" or "Oloroso" → low f6.

A useful sanity check: **f3 and f6 are loosely opposed; f2 and f4 are loosely opposed; f1 and f5 are independent (a sake can be both fragrant and dry, e.g. a dry Daiginjo).** Sakenowa explicitly notes that opposing-axis combinations are possible (a sake can be "rich + crisp"), so we permit high-on-both in edge cases.

A final calibration note: Sakenowa's per-brand normalisation means our 0..1 values are best read as **"how strongly should the recommender weight this axis"** when matching the Western descriptor against the sake library, not as a guarantee that any specific Sakenowa-published vector will sit at exactly that point.

## Where sources agree and disagree

The strongest **consensus** mappings:

- **Aged koshu ≈ Oloroso / dry sherry.** Stated explicitly by [The Koji Club](https://www.thekojiclub.com/blog/aged), [Decanter](https://www.decanter.com/learn/sake-and-food-pairing-a-beginners-guide-541948/), [The Sake Company](https://www.thesakecompany.com/blogs/sake-drops/koshu-sake-how-aging-changes-flavor), and [Sake Times](https://en.sake-times.com/). Born Koshu (Fukui) is the standard worked example.
- **Sparkling sake ≈ Champagne (méthode traditionnelle).** [Awa Sake Association](https://www.awasake.or.jp/), [Wine Enthusiast](https://www.wineenthusiast.com/culture/wine/champagne-style-sparkling-sake/), [VinePair](https://vinepair.com/articles/japanese-sparkling-sake-guide/), and [Sake Times](https://en.sake-times.com/deep-dive/sake_vs_wine_pairing) all converge.
- **Kimoto / Yamahai ≈ peated single-malt Scotch.** [Time Out](https://www.timeout.com/newyork/bars/sake-school-six-sake-types-for-wine-drinkers-scotch-sluggers-and-more), [Whisky Advocate](https://whiskyadvocate.com/the-whisky-lovers-guide-to-sake), and [Izakaya Impressions](https://izakayaimpressions.com/blogs/news/discovering-yamahai-and-kimoto-japans-bold-sake-traditions) align — though Whisky Advocate clarifies the analogy is *umami funk*, not literal phenols.
- **Junmai Daiginjo ≈ aromatic white wine (Riesling / white Burgundy / cool-climate Chardonnay).** [Tippsy / Palate Project](https://palateproject.co/blogs/post/sake-recommendations-for-wine-drinkers), [Heavensake](https://heavensake.com/), [WinedealsBlog](https://www.winedeals.com/blog/post/sake-for-wine-lovers), and [Limonadier](https://limonadier.co/en/blogs/the-spirits-review/verre-sake-nu-origine) agree. Sources split on *which* aromatic white — Riesling (most common), Chardonnay (Limonadier, Heavensake), or Sauvignon Blanc (Time Out, WHWC).

The strongest **disagreements**:

- **Junmai ≈ Cabernet Sauvignon vs Junmai ≈ Pinot Noir.** [Time Out](https://www.timeout.com/newyork/bars/sake-school-six-sake-types-for-wine-drinkers-scotch-sluggers-and-more) maps Junmai to "full-bodied Cabernet Sauvignon"; [Woodland Hills Wine Co.](https://whwc.com/blog/sake-wine-pairing) treats Junmai Ginjo as an aged-Pinot analogue. The disagreement is real and reflects which Junmai you pick: a Tedorigawa Junmai earthy/mushroomy → Pinot; a Daishichi Kimoto Junmai dense/savory → Cabernet. **The map resolves this by giving Cabernet a Kimoto/Yamahai Junmai, and Pinot a more terroir-driven Junmai or Junmai Ginjo.**
- **Honjozo ≈ Merlot vs Honjozo ≈ Pinot Grigio.** [Time Out](https://www.timeout.com/newyork/bars/sake-school-six-sake-types-for-wine-drinkers-scotch-sluggers-and-more) says Merlot ("medium-bodied, soft, easy-to-drink"); Gauntner-influenced sources tend toward "easy white" framing. We pick Merlot for body alignment and note the disagreement in the row.
- **Nigori ≈ dessert wine vs Nigori ≈ horchata / rice pudding.** [Time Out](https://www.timeout.com/newyork/bars/sake-school-six-sake-types-for-wine-drinkers-scotch-sluggers-and-more) calls it "the dessert wine of sake"; [whatsake](https://www.whatsake.com/post/nigori-sake-cloudy-creamy-guide) and [Virginia Philip Academy](https://www.virginiaphilipacademy.com/sake/nigori) push toward creamy non-wine analogues. Both are defensible; we map sweet Nigori to off-dry/dessert wine and dry Nigori (e.g. Kamoizumi "Summer Snow") to a non-wine analogue.

Where the literature is **thin**:

- **Sake ↔ tequila / mezcal.** There is no canonical mapping. Mezcal's smoke has no direct sake correlate; the only honest analogy is *taruzake* (cedar-aged sake) for "wood-driven aromatic complexity", and that's a stretch.
- **Sake ↔ baijiu.** Baijiu's intensity (often 50%+ ABV) and qu-derived funk has no real sake analogue. The closest is *koshu* or *bodaimoto Junmai* on funk grounds only.
- **Sake ↔ gin.** Gin's juniper/botanical aromatics don't transfer to sake. The least-wrong map is a particularly aromatic Daiginjo (high f1) — but the reasoning is shared aromatic intensity, not shared aroma quality.
- **Sake ↔ IPA.** IPA hop bitterness has no f-axis. The map uses high-aroma Junmai Ginjo (banana/pear esters) as the closest analogue to a hazy/NEIPA — an analogy that survives only on aroma, not bitterness.

These thin rows are flagged in the source column.

## Limitations and known failure modes

1. **Sake has no tannin.** Any "tannic" descriptor (Cabernet, Nebbiolo, Barolo, oxidised orange wine) is being projected onto f2 (mellow/umami) + f3 (body) + low f6 (not crisp). The disclaimer is doing real work in those rows.
2. **Sake has lower acidity than wine.** A 6.5 pH sake vs 3.5 pH red wine ([VinePair / Sake Times](https://vinepair.com/articles/japanese-sparkling-sake-guide/)) means "crisp" doesn't mean the same thing. The f6 axis tracks finish-crispness, not acidic snap.
3. **Sake is not bitter the way IPA is bitter.** Hop α-acid bitterness has no sake correlate. Some Junmai (Junmai bitter-finish dry styles like Kid 紀土) carries a quasi-bitter dry finish, but it isn't IPA.
4. **Sake is not phenolic the way peated whisky is phenolic.** Lagavulin's smoke is peat phenol; Kimoto's "smoke" is oxidative + amino-acid funk. The analogy is *evocative*, not chemical.
5. **Sake's "sweetness" is amino-acid-mediated, not residual-sugar-only.** Even high-f5 (dry) sake can taste rounded because of umami; a "dry sake" is not as bracingly dry as a Brut Nature Champagne.
6. **Per-brand normalisation in Sakenowa.** Our 0..1 numbers are intended as recommender weights, not as predictions of Sakenowa's published vector for any specific bottle.
7. **Brand examples drift.** Sake brands release vintage variations, seasonal nama, and new SKUs. The brand listed is the *house style anchor*, not a specific bottle.
